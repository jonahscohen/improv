Bigger Day-to-Day Swings in Step Counts Linked to Lower Reported Energy, Study Finds

A new observational study followed more than 1,200 adults over the age of 50 for six months and found that people whose daily step counts varied sharply from one day to the next reported somewhat lower average energy levels than people with steadier activity patterns. The study does not show that uneven activity causes lower energy, and it cannot rule out the reverse.

Health Desk Explainer Team - not medical advice - July 28, 2026

Study context

Researchers at the Ashford Institute for Population Health, an independent research group, recruited 1,247 adults ages 50 to 74 from three community health networks. Each participant wore a consumer step-count wearable for six months and completed a brief weekly energy and mood questionnaire.

The team compared each person's day-to-day step count variability, meaning how much a given day's total differed from that person's own recent average, against their average self-reported energy score over the same period. The study appeared in the Journal of Behavioral Health Studies, an independent-review publication, and was funded by a general research grant with no involvement from any commercial step-tracking or wellness company.

Key findings

  • Participants whose daily step counts varied by more than about 40 percent from one day to the next reported energy scores roughly 15 percent lower, on average, than participants with more consistent day-to-day totals.
  • The association held after researchers adjusted for total weekly step volume. In other words, it was not simply that less active people reported lower energy; the swings, not just the totals, tracked with lower reported energy.
  • The pattern appeared in both men and women in the sample and across a range of starting fitness levels, though the study was not large enough to confirm it holds equally for every subgroup.
  • Energy scores were based on how participants described their own energy, not on a clinical or physiological measurement.

What the study does not prove

  • It does not show that uneven activity causes lower energy. This is an observational study: it can find a pattern between two things without establishing that one causes the other.
  • It does not rule out the reverse explanation, that people who already feel low on energy on a given day may naturally move around less, which would produce more variability rather than result from it.
  • It does not account for every likely confounder. Sleep quality, chronic illness, and caregiving responsibilities were not measured, and any of them could plausibly affect both step patterns and reported energy.
  • It followed one group of adults aged 50 to 74 in three community networks for six months. The results may not apply to younger adults, to people managing serious illness, or over different time periods.
  • It relied on self-reported energy and consumer wearables, both of which introduce their own margins of error.

Expert commentary

"This is a careful piece of observational work, and the researchers were appropriately restrained in how they framed it. The honest reading is that we now have a pattern worth studying further with a design that can test cause and effect, not a reason to tell anyone how to structure their week."

Dr. Renata Osei, epidemiologist, not involved in the study

"We were surprised the variability measure held up after we controlled for total activity. But I want to be clear that this is one study in one population over six months. We are not in a position to recommend a specific activity pattern based on these results alone."

Dr. Marcus Feld, the study's lead author

Practical takeaways

  • If you already track your steps, treat this study as a reason for curiosity, not alarm. A single observational finding is not grounds for changing an established routine.
  • Talk with a doctor or physical therapist before making any deliberate change to your activity pattern, especially if you manage a chronic condition.
  • Consistency in daily movement is a reasonable general goal on its own merits, apart from this particular study, since it tends to be easier to sustain than sporadic bursts of high activity.
  • Be skeptical of any headline that turns this kind of finding into a firm rule. The study itself does not support that level of certainty.
